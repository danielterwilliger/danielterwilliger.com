Hello, Supercast

I'm Daniel Terwilliger. Lisa told me I should show you my connection to podcasts. Here's who I am, what I do, and my history with this medium.

Podcasts are personal. There's an intimacy to wearing headphones and being the only one listening. It's the only medium where I catch myself doing chores in service of the content: I want to listen to this, so let me go do the dishes. It's not something you put on the TV while you scroll your phone. It can take your whole focus, and it can change the way you think.

A kid with an iPod

I grew up in a big family that moved around a lot. The year we spent in Montreal, I was a teenager and I was isolated, and that's when I dove in. I didn't have a Mac. I fantasized about having one. What I had was an iPod Classic, a Christmas gift, the one with the color screen and the big scroll wheel, and it was my portal to everything else. I listened to MacBreak Weekly, a show about the computers I didn't own, and followed Leo Laporte's network into tech and video game podcasts. iTunes handled the shows Apple knew about. Anything else, I hunted down the raw RSS feed and added it by hand.

I understand how parasocial relationships form, because these people were basically my friends. Intimacy at a distance, for free, over an open feed. That's what this medium does, and I felt it before I could name it.

The thread I followed

The show I refreshed for was MacBreak Weekly, and the reason was Merlin Mann. I've followed Merlin's shows ever since: Back to Work on 5by5, Roderick on the Line, Reconcilable Differences on Relay FM. My father pointed me at Radiolab (I've heard every episode, some more than once) and then This American Life, which became a staple. I still keep every episode of You Look Nice Today downloaded, because some things are relistenable.

The Sunday show

The show I never miss is Le Show with Harry Shearer. It was my father's favorite. Every Sunday, sometimes falling asleep to it, waking up and rewinding. Since my father passed last year, catching Le Show every week is one of the ways I keep that connection alive.

What I believed, and how it changed

When Overcast came along I jumped from Apple's app immediately. It was a product that cared about podcast listeners. When Spotify started pulling shows onto its platform, I felt what most of the community felt: podcasts are supposed to be open and free. RSS, no gates. If a streaming service takes a show down, you lose your episodes. It's the same fight as owned files versus streaming access everywhere else in media. The economics of that era had a strange upside, too. The money mostly wasn't there, so the only shows that survived were passion projects, and that passion is how you got great shows without grifters. The sustainable version was the public radio model, the one networks like 5by5 and Relay FM run on: if you love a thing you can pay for it, but it stays free either way. Something about long-form audio wants to be treated that way.

I felt all of that a lot more strongly five years ago. I still see the downsides of closed platforms. But the Joe Rogan deal is a huge part of why the coming generation knows podcasts exist at all (most of them don't know the name comes from the iPod), and "install an app and you're listening" reaches people a raw RSS feed never will. My cousin just showed me Pocket FM: serialized audio, gamified, a lot of it AI-narrated. I expected to hate it and instead I found it interesting. There's a lot of experimentation left to happen in this space. I don't know whether a raw RSS feed is still the best answer. I know the impact it had on me at the time, and I know that treating premium delivery as a settled question, in either direction, is a mistake. It's a discovery question.

I've paid for exactly one podcast

In fifteen-plus years of listening, I've supported exactly one show with money: Roderick on the Line, on Patreon. I'm the public radio listener. I'd usually rather hear the ad reads, which at this point feel like part of the show. Catching up on a backlog right now, the old ads are archaeology: these were the services that were big in 2023.

So what converted me, once? The show became personal. John ran for Seattle City Council in 2015. The thing felt real, and paying felt like supporting something real, not buying access to content. I'm your hardest listener to convert, and I converted exactly once, for relationship reasons. Understanding people like me, and the much easier listeners who aren't like me, is discovery work.

I also made one

In school, a friend and I started a podcast. Three or four episodes. We recorded a Google call, converted it through YouTube to strip the audio out, and had no good way to host it. It ended partly from scheduling, mostly from friction. I think the episodes were good, for the people who wanted them. But it's a lot of work, and we would rather have spent that time talking to each other while playing games. Making even a few episodes left me with real respect for the people who do it every week, and it showed me the creator-side tooling gap from the inside. Closing that gap is the business Supercast is in.

Podcasts taught me how to work

The early episodes of Back to Work changed how I operate. Episode 3 is literally titled "The Second Arrow": the Buddhist parable where the first arrow is the thing that happens and the second arrow is your reaction to it, and the second one is the one you can do something about. Episode 1 was about the lizard brain, the way it makes work things feel scarier than they are. There was never an alligator in the bathroom; nothing in the world changed except your perception of it. The lesson I kept: you're just a person in a room. And the mechanics I still run my work by started there: capture everything, get tasks out of your head and into a system you trust, know what done looks like. Over the years that hardened into my own rule, half GTD and half Scrum: a task doesn't exist unless it has a due date, a description of done, and an owner. A show I found through an iPod as a lonely teenager is materially why I manage products the way I do.

The listener, today

I'm on Android now. It breaks my heart that Overcast isn't there, but I understand. I tried Spotify and it didn't have everything, so it's Pocket Casts, free tier. Not thrilled, but it's fine. Life is busier with a kid, so the rotation has a hard core: Le Show is the one I always make sure I catch. Reconcilable Differences, catching up. Backlogs in progress: Roderick on the Line, This American Life, and Back to Work, which quietly ended in 2024, so the backlog is finite. Radiolab is there when I need it, and More Perfect is exactly right for the current Supreme Court moment. Listening lives where it always has: dishes, driving, chores, sometimes falling asleep.

One more observation: live streaming on Twitch and YouTube and Kick has become some of what podcasting used to be. Someone on in the background, the feeling that you're there with them. I've joined show communities (Critical Role's Discord absolutely counts). The intimacy moved. It didn't disappear. Where it goes next is an open question.

The other half

That's my history with the medium. Here's the professional half. As I understand it, you're hiring a founding PM to add the bookends around a shipping process that already works: discovery and idea validation before a project starts, delivery and adoption after it ships. That happens to be the shape of my last five years. Three stories, told honestly.

Discovery: the one I got wrong

At eXp, the Director of Transactions and the Director of Agent Operations came to me together with a vague ask: we need a way to keep agents connected, and it has to live in our enterprise system. From that I created ConnectID: flexible groups outside the official contractual team structure, built in Mendix, where anybody could look up what a given Connect ID was for. It was well architected for what was asked, and the demo landed great. A month after launch, two groups had moved over while dozens of teams were still coming in on spreadsheets. The real requirement was hidden under the feature asks: leadership was afraid of losing the incoming teams, and the tracking spreadsheets were failing audits. A sharper product with fewer bells and whistles, wired into onboarding and compliance, would have delivered exactly what they were looking for. I consider it a failure I own, even though it shipped and looked like a delivery, because navigating to the truth of the ask was my job. Delivery isn't adoption. If I had been a better product manager at the time, I would have found the real pain sooner. Now I go find it first.

Adoption: measured the hard way

The flip side is what happens after release. When I turned a hundred scattered VoIP accounts into eXp INBND, an AI-supported call centre, I measured adoption against reality: roughly six months after launch, call handling was 80% higher than the cumulative total across all the old accounts, verified from vendor bills on one side and the Twilio console plus our own dashboard on the other. The change was visible in the business. State brokers finally received the call volume that had always been meant for them, to the point where some hired staff to handle it. And the intake process I built for one team spread across the organization on its own: Legal, Accounting, and every team doing solution delivery ended up running their intake through it. Products earn adoption when they live inside a workflow. I've watched it happen and I've watched it not happen, and I know which decisions made the difference.

And the AI-native part

Supercast builds for a medium in the middle of reinventing itself, so it matters that I work AI-first. At eXp I built the developer workflow that turned meetings into managed work: transcripts into the best model available, Jira issues drafted automatically, refined in loops where a human makes every call. I ran the workshops that taught engineers and support staff to run AI in their terminals. By the time I left, the organization was standardizing user stories on a markdown format written for AI, a standard I feel I influenced. A public working version of the practice lives at github.com/danielterwilliger/ai-oncall, and this site itself keeps an agent-readable layer at danielterwilliger.com/llms.txt.
